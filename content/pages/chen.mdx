> "有犬百余，亦置一槽共食，一犬不至，群犬亦皆不食。"
> "They kept more than a hundred dogs, fed together at a single trough — if even one dog was missing, the rest would not eat."
> — *History of Song, Biography of Chen Jing* (《宋史·陈兢传》), describing the household of the Yimen Chen

## 📖 Introduction

The **Yimen Chen (义门陈, "Chen of the Righteous Gate")** lineage represents one of the most extraordinary experiments in communal living and Confucian family ethics in Chinese history. For **332 years (731–1063 CE)**, spanning **fifteen generations**, the family lived as a single household in Jiangzhou (江州, present-day De'an County, Jiangxi Province) — sharing property, meals, labor, and childcare, with no private wealth and no separate kitchens: "室无私财，厨无别爨" (*"no private wealth in any room, no separate stove in any kitchen"*).

At its height the household numbered **more than 3,900 members**, and family records say contemporaries called it "天下第一家" — *the First Family under Heaven*. Its story is not merely family lore: it is recorded in the official *History of Song* (《宋史·陈兢传》), and emperors of three successive courts — the Tang, the Southern Tang, and the Song — repeatedly honored the family (the genealogy counts **nine emperors and twenty-seven commendations**). Its self-written constitution, the **33 Family Rules** of 890 CE, was copied into the imperial archives by order of Emperor Taizong of Song and distributed to princes and ministers as a model of family governance. Its school, the **Dongjia Academy (东佳书院)**, was among the earliest academies in China, older than the famous White Deer Grotto Academy by roughly half a century.

The story ends with a paradox worthy of its scale: in 1062 the family had grown so large, so admired, and so influential that the emperor himself — urged by ministers including the celebrated judge **Bao Zheng (包拯)** — issued an edict asking it to divide. The 291 branches that scattered across the empire carried the family's name to every province, which is why it is still said that "天下陈氏出江州" — *"All Chen families under heaven trace back to Jiangzhou."*

## 🌊 Deep Roots: The Lineage Before Jiangzhou

The Jiangzhou settlement of 731 was not a beginning from nothing. The family's own genealogies trace an unbroken line from the sage-king **Shun (舜帝)** down to the founders of Yimen — a chain that runs through some of the most storied names of early Chinese history:

- **Emperor Shun (舜帝)** — the sage-king; his descendants took the surname Gui (妫).
- **Gui Man, Duke Hu of Chen (陈胡公妫满)** — in 1046 BCE, King Wu of Zhou enfeoffed this descendant of Shun at Wanqiu (宛丘, present-day Huaiyang, Henan), founding the **State of Chen (陈国)**. His descendants took the state's name as their surname: this is the origin of the Chen (陈) surname itself.
- **Chen Wan (陈完)** — fled a court intrigue in Chen to the State of Qi in 672 BCE and changed his surname to Tian (田); his descendants eventually ruled Qi, one of the Seven Warring States.
- **Chen Ping (陈平)** — the brilliant strategist whose "six ingenious plans" helped Liu Bang found the Han dynasty; chancellor under three reigns.
- **Chen Shi (陈实, 104–186 CE)** — magistrate of Taiqiu in the Eastern Han, the moral exemplar of **Yingchuan (颍川)**. Two beloved idioms trace to him: the "gentleman on the beam" (梁上君子), from his gentle reform of a burglar hiding in his rafters, and the "gathering of virtuous stars" (德星聚会) — when court astronomers reported an auspicious stellar convergence, it was traced to Chen Shi's family gathering with the scholar Xun Shu. Hence the ancestral hall names still used by Chen families today: **颍川堂 (Yingchuan Hall)**, **德星堂 (Hall of the Virtuous Star)**, and **聚星堂 (Hall of Gathered Stars)**.
- **Chen Baxian (陈霸先, 503–559)** — founder of the **Chen dynasty** (557–589), the only Chinese dynasty to bear its ruling family's surname.
- **Chen Shuming, Prince of Yidu (宜都王陈叔明, 555–618)** — sixth son of Emperor Xuan of Chen (陈宣帝). After the dynasty fell to the Sui, this princely line survived in official service. It is from Chen Shuming that the Yimen Chen descend directly: 叔明 → 志高 → 才 → 蕴珪 → and finally the brothers **陈兼 (Chen Jian)** and **陈旺 (Chen Wang)**.

*Line of descent per the family genealogies; scholarly sources note minor discrepancies in generational order between the Song-era accounts.*

## 🏛️ Founding: Two Streams, One Family

**Founded:** 731 CE (Tang dynasty, 19th year of the Kaiyuan era)
**Location:** Yongqing Village, Changle Li, Taiping Township, Putang Precinct (蒲塘场), Xunyang County, Jiangzhou — present-day **Yimen Chen Village, Cheqiao Town, De'an County, Jiangxi**
**Founder:** Chen Wang (陈旺)

In 724, **Chen Jian (陈兼)** — fifth-generation descendant of the Prince of Yidu — passed the imperial *jinshi* examination and was posted to Jiangzhou. His younger brother **Chen Wang** followed him there, and when Chen Jian was transferred onward, Chen Wang stayed. In 731 he bought land and founded a family estate at Changle Li. From him the line ran 旺 → 机 → 感 → 蓝 → 青 — five generations that three centuries later would be posthumously ennobled as five dukes by Emperor Renzong of Song.

The family's other stream descended from Chen Jian himself, through his son Chen Jing (陈京, *jinshi* 766). Its most famous figure was **Chen Boxuan (陈伯宣, 824–890)**, a hermit-scholar of Mount Lu whose annotated edition of Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* circulated in his day, and who declined repeated court summonses (he was granted the title of Editorial Director, 著作佐郎). In 877, when the Huang Chao rebellion reached Jiangzhou — the warlord Liu Yanzhang sacked the city (as recorded in the *Zizhi Tongjian*) — Chen Boxuan moved his family inland to De'an and joined Chen Wang's descendants:

> "异流同源，合族同处" — *Two streams from one source, united as a single clan.*

This union of the two branches created the household that would astonish the empire. Seven years later came the first imperial recognition:

👑

**📜 First Imperial Commendation (884 CE)**
In the 4th year of Zhonghe, **Emperor Xizong of Tang** personally inscribed a plaque reading **"义门陈氏" ("The Chen Clan of the Righteous Gate")**, and bestowed a couplet:

> "九重天上旌书贵，千古人间义字香。"
> *"From the nine-fold heavens, the emperor's decree of honor is precious; through a thousand ages, the word 'righteousness' perfumes the human world."*

A poem attributed to the same emperor asks: "问道江南谁第一？咸称唯有义门陈" — *"Ask who ranks first south of the Yangtze — all reply: none but the Yimen Chen."* Because the commendation was won in his lifetime and largely through his renown, Chen Boxuan is honored in the genealogies as the clan's "illustrious ancestor of the Righteous Gate."

## ⚖️ Governance: A Constitution for a Clan

By 890 the household was already seven generations deep and several hundred strong. That year — the 1st year of Dashun — Chen Boxuan's son **Chen Chong (陈崇)**, a *jinshi* who rose to be Chief Administrator of Jiangzhou (江州长史), did something without precedent: he gave his family a written constitution, the **33 Family Rules (义门陈氏家法三十三条)**. His preface states the problem and the principle with striking clarity — fearing that "as our descendants multiply, the wise and the foolish will differ," he set down rules whose spirit was:

> "推功任能，惩恶劝善" — *"Advance the meritorious, employ the able; punish wrong, encourage good."*
> "须令均等，务求和同" — *"All must be equal, that all may be in harmony."*

### How the household governed itself

The 33 Rules read like the charter of a small republic *(all provisions below are from the original text, preserved in the family genealogies)*:

- **An elected executive.** One steward (主事) and two deputies ran all internal and external affairs — chosen "**不拘长少，但择谨慎才能之人任之**" (*"without regard to age or seniority — select only the prudent and capable"*), and replaceable: "若才能不称，仍须择人代之" (*"if his ability proves unequal, another must be chosen in his place"*). Later commentators have called this one of the earliest merit-based selection systems in Chinese family governance.
- **Separated financial power.** Two treasurers (库司) — "公平刚毅之人" (*fair-minded and resolute*) — controlled all receipts, disbursements, rewards, and penalties. Every estate had its own foreman, whose accounts were audited at year's end.
- **Specialized offices**: a registrar of marriages and birth order (勘司), ten storehouse keepers (宅库人) for granaries, gardens, and gates, two members assigned to **study medicine** for the household, and eight daughters-in-law rostered to the communal kitchen.
- **Communal meals in ordered sittings.** The whole family ate together in the great hall: men under forty at the first sitting (so they could get to work), elders with the family head at the second; women dined in the rear hall in the same order.
- **Absolute economic equality.** All market transactions required itemized accounts settled with the treasury. Clothing was issued by season on fixed quotas; even hemp shoes (three pairs a year) and sewing money were standardized.
- **Strict monogamy.** Each man was allowed one wife and "**一室不得置畜仆隶**" — no concubines, no personal servants. (The *History of Song* corroborates: "不畜仆妾" — *they kept neither servants nor concubines*.)
- **A graded penal code**, administered at the family's own Discipline Hall (刑杖厅): five strokes for minor misconduct, ten for drunken offense to others, fifteen plus one year's forfeiture of clothing allowance and demotion to labor for defying the family head or gambling, twenty plus three years for corruption or debauchery — with restoration in every case for those who reformed ("改则复之").
- **Universal education** (see the Academy section): every child entered school at seven and studied to fifteen, with the gifted sent on to Dongjia for advanced study.

The 33 Rules were flanked by two shorter texts also credited to Chen Chong: the **Twelve Family Standards (义门家范十二则)** — verses on revering the court, honoring ancestors, filial piety, brotherly harmony, husband and wife, teaching children, honoring teachers, choosing friends, clan solidarity, good neighborliness, equal distribution, and diligence — and the **Eight Family Instructions (家训八则)**: 孝悌忠信礼义廉耻 — *filial piety, brotherhood, loyalty, trustworthiness, propriety, righteousness, integrity, and a sense of shame*.

📜

**Into the Imperial Archives (996–997 CE)**
When the palace commissioner Pei Yu presented the family's code at court, **Emperor Taizong of Song** exclaimed: "天下有此人家，真良家也！" (*"That such a family exists under heaven — a truly virtuous family!"*). He ordered the **33 Rules copied into the imperial archives (史馆)** and distributed to princes and high officials, one copy each, "that all might know the way of filial piety and righteousness."

### Institutions ahead of their time

Family records and modern researchers of Yimen culture credit the household with a remarkable set of communal institutions *(genealogy accounts; individual dates vary slightly across sources)*:

- **百婴堂 (Hall of a Hundred Infants)** — a communal nursery beside the silkworm workshops; any nursing mother fed any crying child, "whether her own or not."
- **寿安堂 (Hall of Longevity and Peace, 1023)** — a retirement hall furnished with musical instruments, chess, and books for the household's elders, with a separate residence for members returning from official posts.
- **医俗院 (the family clinic, Northern Song era)** — staffed by physicians recruited from various regions, serving the household and outside villagers alike.
- **A granary (义仓) for the imperial relief grain, thirty-two communal wells, a textile and sericulture yard (都蚕院)** with its own incentive system for silk production.
- **义门通宝 — the family's own coin.** The genealogies record that the household was uniquely authorized to mint a circulating bronze coin inscribed "义门通宝"; when the patriot-statesman **Wen Tianxiang (文天祥)** wrote a preface for a branch genealogy in 1277, he made a point of recording this "righteous coinage minted by imperial leave" (奉诏铸义钱). *(Family records; presented here as clan tradition.)*

Two stories from this chronicle deserve telling in full:

🕊️

**One Pigeon, Three Thousand Mouths (993)**
After the audience of 993, Taizong tested the family's famed equality with a gift: a single imperial pigeon. Chen Jing carried it home, minced it into a pot of wine, and shared the broth among the entire household of thousands — so that everyone might "taste the emperor's favor." When the imperial envoy reported this, the emperor sighed: "诚哉，义门也！" — *"Truly, a Righteous Gate indeed!"* *(Family records.)*

🌾

**Taking Only Half (999)**
When the court renewed the annual grain loan of three thousand *dan*, family head **Chen Xu (陈旭)** accepted only half, saying the family could stretch its own stores to the harvest. Urged to take the full amount and profit from the surplus, he refused: "岂可见利忘义而为罔上之事乎？" — *"How could we chase profit, forget righteousness, and deceive the throne?"* Emperor Zhenzong praised him and bestowed a poem. *(Family records.)*

## 🎓 Dongjia Academy (东佳书院)

The family's school is a chapter of Chinese educational history in its own right — by the family's chronology, roughly **half a century older than the White Deer Grotto Academy**, and listed by modern historians of education among the four Tang-era academies with documented teaching activity.

**Its evolution, traced in the genealogies:**

1. **义门别墅 (Yimen Villa, 885)** — a guest lodge called 寄咏舒 built at scenic Dongjia ("East Excellence") to house the scholars and officials who flocked to the newly honored family.
2. **东佳书楼 (Dongjia Library, 889)** — a three-story library ("文范堂" hall below, "百科书斋" studies in the middle, "御书阁" imperial-books pavilion above) holding a collection contemporaries ranked extraordinary: "**所藏帖与书，号为天下第一**" — *its books and calligraphy were called the finest under heaven*.
3. **陈氏书堂 (Chen Family School, 890)** — formalized in the 33 Rules as a two-tier system: a primary school (书屋) by the residence, where "every child enters at seven and leaves at fifteen," and the advanced hall at Dongjia for those preparing for the imperial examinations. Tuition, paper, brushes, and ink were all provided by the family treasury.
4. **东佳书院 (Dongjia Academy, early Song)** — at its height "正堂九架，庑房四百" (*a great hall of nine bays and four hundred chambers*), with "堂庑数十间，聚书数千卷，田二十顷，以为游学之资" (*dozens of halls, thousands of volumes, and twenty* qing *of endowed land to support traveling students* — Xu Kai, *Record of the Chen Family School*). The endowed **study-fields (学田)** are considered China's earliest recorded school endowment of the kind.
5. **After the division** — the academy outlived the household itself: relocated to the De'an county seat in 1102–1106 as the **义门书院 (Yimen Academy)**, later rebuilt as the Hedong Academy (1557) and Fuyang Academy (1823), until 1903, when it was converted into De'an County's modern higher primary school — an educational lineage of **over a thousand years**.

Crucially, the academy was **open to outsiders**: "延四方学者，伏腊皆资焉" — *scholars from all quarters were welcomed, and supported through winter and summer alike*. As the *Book of Southern Tang* records, "**江南名士皆肄业于其家**" — *the notable scholars of the South all studied at their school.* Song statesman Qian Ruoshui left the picture in verse:

> "居住东佳对白云，义风深可劝人伦。儿童尽得诗书力，门巷偏多车马尘。"
> *"Dwelling at Dongjia, facing the white clouds — a righteous air that summons men to virtue; every child here draws strength from the classics, and the lane is thick with the dust of visiting carriages."*

The results showed at the examination hall: in **1044** alone, family records count **403 members sitting the imperial examinations and 45 passing**, with eighteen serving at court and twenty-nine as prefects, magistrates, and military administrators. The academy's later admirers ranged from the Song statesman Yang Yi, who held Dongjia up as a model when writing of other academies, to the Japanese scholar Hiraoka Kenji, who called it "a prototype of the ancient Chinese academy"; in 2009 the academy entered a Jiangxi Province senior-high literature textbook.

> "八百头牛耕日月，三千灯火读文章。"
> *"Eight hundred oxen plough through the days and months; three thousand lamps burn late over books."*
> — a Song-era description of Yimen's farming-and-studying way of life

## 🏞️ Yimen at Its Height

**The household kept growing for three centuries** *(figures from the dynastic history and family records; the sources differ slightly)*:

- **700+** members in the mid-tenth century (the *History of Song* records "长幼七百余口" under family head Chen Fang 陈昉, who presided over the household for thirty-five years)
- **740** members in 974
- **1,478** members in 1000
- **3,900+** members by 1033 — the figure at which it stood when ordered to divide

By the mid-eleventh century the estate had become a small city. Within a radius of some twenty *li* around the Righteous Gate stood the imperial-books tower (御书楼), the commendation terrace (旌表台), the Virtuous Star tower (德星楼), the great dining hall (馈食堂) and assembly hall (广堂), the discipline hall (刑杖厅), the sericulture yard, the clinic, the nursery, the elders' hall, the reception halls for visiting officials, thirty-two wells — and the famous **hundred-dog kennel (百犬牢)**, whose communal trough entered the *History of Song* itself. Family life within it produced lines that still startle:

> "堂前架上衣无主，三岁孩儿不识母。"
> *"On the racks before the hall, the clothes belong to no one; a child of three does not single out its own mother."* — because children were nursed and raised in common

Tradition also tells of **击鼓传餐** — the drum on Dagu Hill beating out the summons to meals for the whole settlement *(clan tradition; scholars note that in later years, with estates scattered across two counties, distant farms must have kept their own kitchens on a common standard)*. And the family chronicle records the origin of a field called **公婆丘 ("Grandparents' Field")**: purchased on New Year's Eve of 911 — the same night thirty-seven boys were born to the various branches, all of whom, the chronicle claims, later passed the imperial examinations.

Prosperity, however, was always thinner than it looked. The very equality that held the family together — everyone fed from one treasury, commerce forbidden as morally suspect — kept it permanently on the edge of hunger. Hu Dan's *Record of the Righteous Gate* (1002) admits that in lean years "举宗啜粥，杂以藻菜" — *the whole clan drank thin gruel mixed with water-plants*. From 990 onward the household depended on annual grain loans from the state; in 1044 it had to send 1,400 members to live at its Huangzhou estates, and in 1058 those far-flung members built five hundred small rooms — the **回归院 ("Court of Return")** — to house their homecomings at festival times.

## 🌍 The Great Division (1062–1063)

In 1062, ministers **Wen Yanbo (文彦博), Bao Zheng (包拯), Fan Shidao (范师道), and Lü Hui (吕诲)** jointly memorialized the throne: the Yimen Chen had grown too large, too concentrated, and too prominent — for the family's own preservation, it should divide. In the seventh month, **Emperor Renzong** consented, and issued what the genealogy preserves as one of the gentlest edicts ever to dissolve anything. It reasons rather than commands, and closes:

> "鸟兽有异巢之日，烽茧有散处之时；物类犹然合离，人生岂可长群？"
> *"Birds and beasts have their day of parting nests; even cocoons in the boiling pot must scatter at last. If all things in nature join and part, how can human beings remain forever gathered?"*

**The process took nine months** (July 1062 – March 1063). The emperor dispatched Xie Jingchu (谢景初), Fiscal Commissioner of the Jiangnan West Circuit, with five other officials to supervise the partition in person. At Xie's own petition — moved, the record says, by the sight of kinsmen unable to let go of one another — the court first conferred honorary stipends on twelve stewards of the fifteenth generation, one for each of the twelve generational characters bestowed in 997: 冕 (知成郎), 暹 (守成郎), 晟 (宗成郎), 星 (希成郎), 旻 (公成郎), 显 (汝成郎), 鼎 (才成郎), 昊 (思成郎), 昇 (彦成郎), 昺 (承成郎), 暠 (延成郎), 昂 (继成郎) — twelve names, the genealogist notes with a smile, every one containing the radical for "sun."

**The estate was divided into 291 branch settlements (庄)**: 110 in Jiangnan, 91 in the adjacent Chu lands (the middle Yangtze), and 90 estates in Zhili, Sichuan, Zhejiang, Guangdong, and Fujian acquired over the years through members' official postings. *(Counting the further partition of home-county lands in Dehua, Ruichang, Xingzi, De'an, and Jianchang into 47 shares, some genealogies give totals of 334 or 338.)* The branches drew lots by generation to determine their destinations across **72 prefectures** — roughly sixteen provinces on the modern map. Two branches, Huoxiang and Zhuling, remained to keep the ancestral shrines. Each departing branch hung above its new gate a plaque reading **"义门世家" ("Family of the Righteous Gate")** — and these plaques, multiplying down the centuries, are the origin of the saying:

🌏

**"天下陈氏出江州"**
*"All Chen families under heaven trace back to Jiangzhou."*

As the branches dispersed, the fifteenth-generation compilers produced the *Imperially Decreed Genealogy of Migration* (the "gold-character genealogy"), for which **Su Shi (苏轼)** — Su Dongpo himself — wrote the inscription: "陈氏遗谱，子孙葆之" — *"The genealogy the Chens leave behind: may their descendants guard it well."* The family's own *Record of the Division* closes with the sentence that every branch genealogy has copied since:

> "祖宗之产虽析，子孙之心莫二……则今日之一义门，后日千百万义门自此始也！"
> *"Though the ancestors' estate is divided, the hearts of the descendants remain one... From today's one Righteous Gate shall spring, in days to come, a million Righteous Gates."*

🍲

**Legend: The Shattered Cauldron (碎锅析庄)**
Folk tradition tells the division differently. Unable to bear deciding the split themselves, the elders hung the household's great iron cooking-cauldron from the beam of the ancestral hall and let it fall. It shattered into 291 pieces — so 291 branches it would be, and each family drew its migration route by how far its shard had flown. *(Folk legend.)*

## 🕯️ After the Division: Ruin and Remembrance

The centuries that followed explain why visitors today find a village and memorial sites rather than a preserved city:

- **1128** — amid the Song-Jin wars, the bandit army of Li Cheng burned and looted the old Yimen estate.
- **Late Song (1270s)** — the Mongol invasion ravaged what had been rebuilt; the Huoxiang caretaker branch finally left the ancestral site.
- **1362–63** — the deepest wound. The rebel emperor **Chen Youliang (陈友谅)** — a Yimen descendant who named his short-lived dynasty "Han" and his era "大义" (*Great Righteousness*) — fought Zhu Yuanzhang for the empire and died at the Battle of Lake Poyang. The victorious Zhu, founding the Ming, took revenge on the Chen: his soldiers dug up the Yimen tombs, smashed the steles, razed the site, and persecuted Yimen descendants across Jiangxi. The great building complex never rose again.
- **1553–54** — in a remarkable turn, Zhu Yuanzhang's own descendant, the **Jiajing Emperor**, ordered the site surveyed and honored: a memorial arch, "义门陈氏遗址" (*Site of the Yimen Chen*), was erected by imperial decree, with commemorative steles by officials and scholars — remembered in clan tradition as the Ming house's stone apology to the family its founder had destroyed.
- **1668** — on Qingming of that year, clan records tell, some three thousand Yimen descendants from across the country gathered at the ancestral site, and in three days and nights of communal labor — sleeping in the open on Chen Wang's tomb-hill — raised a timber shrine they named **重兴祠, the Shrine of Revival**.

## 🌟 Modern Legacy

A thousand years after the division, "Yimen" remains a living identity. The ancestral tombs of Chen Wang, Chen Jing, and Chen Boxuan were restored by branch subscriptions in the 1990s; a research society for Jiangzhou Yimen Chen culture now works to collect and study the dispersed genealogies; and every Qingming and winter solstice, buses arrive at Yimen Chen Village under banners reading "江州义门陈氏××庄祭祖" — *branch such-and-such of the Jiangzhou Yimen Chen, come to honor the ancestors*. In 1993 the centenarian statesman Chen Lifu sent from Taiwan a piece of calligraphy for the clan's genealogy projects: simply, "义门堂."

### Notable Descendants

Genealogical researchers trace many prominent figures of modern Chinese history to the 291 branches of the 1063 division *(as reported by the* Jiangxi Daily *(1994) on the research of the Jiangxi Genealogical Studies Society; attributions of this kind rest on branch genealogies)*:

- **Chen Baozhen (陈宝箴)** — reformist governor of Hunan in the late Qing, and his grandson **Chen Yinke (陈寅恪)** — one of the greatest historians of twentieth-century China (the Yining Chen line, itself descended from a Yimen branch)
- **Chen Duxiu (陈独秀)** — co-founder of the Chinese Communist Party
- **Chen Yun (陈云)** — senior state leader of the People's Republic
- **Chen Yi (陈毅)** — Marshal of the People's Republic of China
- **Chen Geng (陈赓)** — senior general of the People's Liberation Army
- **Chen Cheng (陈诚)** and **Chen Lifu (陈立夫)** — leading figures of the Republic of China

## 🎭 From the Family's Literary Trove

The genealogies preserve eighty-three couplets and some three hundred poems written for the family by emperors, ministers, and its own members. A small selection:

> "萃居三千口人间第一，合爨四百年天下无双。"
> *"Three thousand living as one — first in the human world; four hundred years sharing one hearth — without equal under heaven."*
> — couplet bestowed by Emperor Taizong of Song (997)

> "室无私财方能三千九百口同居义聚……厨无异爨遂使四百八十年共济睦族……"
> *"Only with no private wealth could three thousand nine hundred live in righteous union; only with no separate stoves could four hundred eighty years pass in family concord."*
> — Chen Shu (陈恕), Song Minister of Finance and a son of the family

> "一犬未至百犬不食，牢内异物皆效义；一吠突起百吠齐怒，寨中同声共护门。"
> *"If one dog is absent, a hundred will not eat — even the beasts of the kennel practice righteousness; if one bark rises, a hundred answer as one — with a single voice they guard the gate."*
> — kennel couplet by Chen Jue (陈觉), Southern Tang Minister of War

> "家严三尺法，官省五条刑。"
> *"Where the family's own code is strict, the magistrate's punishments go unused."*
> — Discipline Hall couplet by Chen Du (陈度)

And one last legend, still told at the village *(folk legend)*: when the division was decreed, a great tortoise pushed open the door of the council hall and stood weeping before the elders. Promised that the departing branches would return every year to honor the ancestors, it withdrew to the hillside and waited — waiting still, villagers say, as the tortoise-shaped rock called **千年龟, the Thousand-Year Tortoise**, which greets descendants returning to Yimen Chen Village today.

- 《宋史·陈兢传》 — *History of Song*, Biography of Chen Jing (the household's principal record in official history)
- 徐锴《陈氏书堂记》 (969/1007) — Xu Kai, *Record of the Chen Family School*
- 胡旦《义门记》 (1002) — Hu Dan, *Record of the Righteous Gate*, written during a stay at Dongjia
- 杨亿《御书楼记》 (1005) — Yang Yi, *Record of the Imperial Books Tower*
- 王应麟《玉海》; 释文莹《湘山野录》; 陆游《南唐书》 — Song-era compilations corroborating the imperial gifts and the academy's fame
- 《义门陈氏家法三十三条》 (890) — the 33 Family Rules, with the Twelve Family Standards and Eight Instructions, as preserved in the branch genealogies
- 《夷陵陈氏支系续谱正修稿》 (2018) — family-held genealogy compiling the Jiangzhou Yimen documents cited throughout this page
